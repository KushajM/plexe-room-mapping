How a pair gets a label

A supplier listing is placed on a room from the hotel's own website only when its normalised words equal exactly one published room's words. Two listings on the same room form a same-room pair; two listings on different rooms, where neither room's words contain the other's, form a different-room pair. Anything ambiguous is left out rather than guessed.

Baselines and results

Scores are counted per supplier name, so identical names already share a row; that is why "Identical names together" scores the same as "Every name its own row".

Why the trivial token-set baseline scores near-perfect. v5.1 gold only checks names worded the same as the hotel's own room list: a same-room pair exists only when both names have exactly the words of one published room. Two names with the same set of words are therefore always the same room by construction, so grouping by word set never misses a match this gold can see. The gold cannot see differently worded names for one room, so a low missed-match number here is a floor, not a verdict.

Limitations

  • Missed matches measure only easy splits. v5.1 split numbers see names that differ by word order or punctuation, not differently worded names for the same room.
  • Gold granularity. A hotel's website may list "Deluxe Room" once while suppliers sell king and twin versions; the gold treats them as one room.
  • Machine-made labels. Most pairs come from an exact-words rule, not from people; a person agreed with the related internal gold 80 of 100 times on first pass.
  • Chain hotels are missing. hotels could not be scraped because their chain's website blocks automated access; we did not work around it.
  • Third-party room content. Supplier catalogues that carry Expedia content are used only as internal inputs and are never published here.
  • Coverage. Gold hotels carry of panel traffic; results may not hold for the long tail.
